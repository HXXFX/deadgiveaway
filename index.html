Dead Giveaway

A top-down shooter whose enemy builds a model of how you move and aims where you are about to be. Every panel around the arena is reading that model live while you play.

Why a new Mirror flails at first

It really does start empty — every weight in the layer that decides what it does is zero. With nothing learned, each decision is an even coin flip, so round one opens with it firing, reloading and turning at random. It has your keys and no idea when to press them. It is shaped within seconds: over one minute, with nothing differing but whether the player pulled a trigger, it went from 6 shots to 56. A rival you Continue is the exception — it walks in with everything it learned about you last time, and never flails at all.

Study pause

Between rounds it studies you and practises against a copy of itself. A longer pause means more practice per round — switch it between normal and long in the bar below.

Your data

Nothing is uploaded, ever. The model of you is saved in your browser only, so the Mirror can remember you between sessions — it never leaves your machine. When a saved rival exists the game asks before anything happens: Continue picks the fight back up, Quick play faces a blank Mirror and keeps nothing from that session, and New story erases the rival for good, once you confirm it. That card is the only thing that ever erases the memory — New fight in the bar below just starts a new world and leaves it alone. No account, no tracking, no analytics, no network requests at all.

How it is built

Plain ES modules, no framework and no build step. The 3‑D is drawn by hand on a 2‑D canvas and the network is about a hundred lines of arithmetic. Every room is generated from a seed rather than authored.

How much it has become you

How to read it

The bench: your mask, the copy of it filling to the score, the dial reading the same score, and the three gauges the score is made of — hands, aim, trigger.

The number is an edge, not a score

Each gauge is how far past a control the policy got — the best answer that needs no learning. Beat nothing, score nothing.

hands (45%) is graded at the moments you change keys: a held key predicts itself, so only your decisions can show a style. It unlocks after a hundred key changes — about twenty seconds of ordinary play, longer if you move in long straight lines. If you never move there is nothing to grade, and it stays at zero honestly.

aim (30%) is graded only on frames where you actually turn, against always guessing your commonest direction.

trigger (25%) is how many times more likely it is to call a shot on a frame you shot than on one you did not.

Why it can be trusted

Every frame is graded before it is learned from, so the score is never a memory of its own training set. “Still watching” clears after 600 graded frames.

The number is eased, and here is exactly how

The raw measurement jitters hard enough to read as the Mirror forgetting you, so this dial is smoothed: it climbs quickly toward a rise and is held to three points a second on the way down. What you see is a few seconds behind the raw number, and never a different story from it. Nothing else on the screen is smoothed.

Its controls no lessons yet

How to read it

Your controls, in its hands: the keyboard at the back, the mouse beside it, the trigger and the reload in front. A pressed key sinks, and the fan on the desk is every direction it weighed for the turn it is about to make.

The lights are wants, not states

Each figure is a live probability — how hard it wants that control right now. It decides at a hand’s tempo, about twelve decisions a second, not sixty.

The fair game

It has exactly what you have: the same keys, the same mouse, the same movement and shooting rules, and it reads your health and ammo the same way you read its off the header. Nothing extra, nothing missing.

Its live wants — each key, the turn, the trigger, the reload.

What it sees numbers, live

How to read it

The dome is its whole world. The sixteen fins are its distance feelers, one every 22.5° — tall and gold is open space, short and red is a wall at its shoulder. The pale spoke is where it is facing; the blue mark is you.

A solid line to you means it could shoot you right now; dashed means the line is blocked. Around the dome is a fixed ring, engraved like an instrument bezel: the range and its unit on the lower arc, how long the line has been open on the upper, and a lamp that lights when something is in the air at it. The readings sit on the ring, clear of the turning fins, so nothing the dome does can cross them.

What it is given

This picture is not drawn from the input — it is the input: the 43 numbers the policy receives each frame, live. Nothing here is something you cannot see on your own screen.

Its whole world, live — 43 numbers, and nothing you don’t get too.

What it took off you against a control

How to read it

Three columns on one plate. The plate is the control — the score a thing that learned nothing would get — and each column is what the policy took past it. Zero means no better than the obvious answer.

hands — did it call your new key set at the moments you change keys, against the best no-learning guess. aim — the direction you turned, against your commonest direction. trigger — whether its trigger reads higher on the frames you fired, against firing at your average rate.

“Graded first”

Every frame is scored before it is trained on, so no column is a memory of the training set.

Hands, aim, trigger — how much of each is yours now, past what plain guessing would get.

The policy

How to read it

The actual network, live: 43 numbers in, two hidden layers of 64, and the decisions out — four keys, the choice of turn, the trigger, the reload. The pulse is one decision crossing it, about twelve a second, and its brightness is how much the layer it is passing is actually doing. The marks on each slab are real activations, sampled across the whole layer.

What it does not show

The weights — there are thousands, and any picture of them is a grey rectangle. You are watching the thinking, not the wiring.

All of it

This one policy, plus a small dedicated reload net trained the same way from the same frames, is everything. There is no scripted bot behind it — no orbit, no peek timer, no routing, no cadence written by hand.

Its whole brain, thinking — one pulse per decision, live.

The ledger

How to read it

A staircase of the whole session, one tread a round: the wide blue treads are your kills, the narrow red steps behind them are its. It is kept as it happens, never rebuilt from a summary afterwards.

Why the kills matter

It learns killing from exactly one source: watching you win. If you have never killed it, it has never seen a kill. A round only ends when somebody dies, and between rounds it takes one rehearsal step against a frozen copy of itself and keeps what worked.

Every frame of you it has watched — and the kills the whole game turns on.
